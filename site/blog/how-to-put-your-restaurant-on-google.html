How to Put Your Restaurant on Google

Claim it, set it up, and actually use it. A practical walk through the Google Business Profile features that matter to a restaurant, and the ones owners usually never touch.

If you just opened a restaurant and are wondering how to list your business on Google, or your restaurant already appears but you have no idea who controls it, start here.

Google Business Profile is the listing that can show your restaurant across Google Search and Google Maps. It is free to add or claim. Once the profile is verified, you can manage basic information like your address, phone number and hours, but restaurants get quite a bit more than that: menus, ordering links, reservations, photos, posts, reviews, social links and performance data can all be part of the profile.

The important part is not simply having a Google listing. It is making sure the listing customers find is accurate, current, and sending them where you actually want them to go.

How do I put my restaurant on Google?

First, search Google Maps for your restaurant's exact name and city. There are three different situations, and they should not be treated the same way.

If nothing comes up, you can create a new Business Profile and add your business information. Google will then take you through verification.

If your restaurant already appears but has not been verified, you can claim the existing profile. An authorized owner or manager can find the restaurant in Maps, choose Claim this business, and begin verification.

If the restaurant already has a verified profile controlled by someone else, request access to that profile instead of treating it like a brand-new business. This happens more often than you might expect after a restaurant changes owners, an old employee set up the listing, or a previous marketing company handled it. Google has a formal ownership-request process for exactly this situation.

New restaurant, no profile
→ add it
Exists, unclaimed
→ claim it
Exists, someone else controls it
→ request ownership

Creating a second profile for a restaurant that already has one is the wrong move in all three cases. Duplicates cause exactly the problem you are trying to fix: customers finding the wrong information.

Where you actually manage the profile

This trips people up, because the answer changed.

There is no separate app to download for a single restaurant. Google now has you manage an individual Business Profile directly on Google Search and Google Maps. Sign in with the account that owns the profile, search your own restaurant's name, and the management options appear in the profile itself.

Businesses running many locations can still manage them together in one place, but for one restaurant, searching your own name while signed in is the front door.

One more thing worth knowing before you start editing: changes are not always instant. Google notes it can take 24 to 48 hours for edits and uploaded photos to show on Search and Maps. If you fix your hours and they look unchanged an hour later, that is usually normal rather than broken.

Use an account the restaurant controls

This part is our recommendation, not a Google requirement: the restaurant itself should retain ownership of its Business Profile.

Google allows owners to add other owners and managers without handing over the primary account. Managers can handle everyday work such as profile edits and review responses, while ownership remains with the business.

That means your social media person, manager or marketing company does not need to own your restaurant's Google presence just to work on it.

Keep the keys. Give people access when they need it.

How do I verify my restaurant on Google?

Verification proves to Google that you are authorized to manage the business.

Google documents several verification methods: phone or text, email, live video call, video recording, and mail (a postcard with a code). Live video call and video recording are two different things, and postcard verification is not available for every business. Depending on the restaurant and the information Google already has, you may be asked to complete more than one step.

The part owners find frustrating is worth stating plainly: you do not get to pick. Google says verification methods are determined automatically and cannot be changed. Which options you see depends on your business type, your public information, your region and your hours. Where video verification is available, Google recommends taking it.

If you land on video recording, know what it involves before you start, because this is where restaurants get stuck. The video has to be captured live inside the Business Profile flow on a mobile device. You cannot film it in advance and upload it. Google asks for a single unedited recording of at least thirty seconds that shows where the business is, proof the business exists, and proof you manage it. In practice that means walking from a street sign or building number to your own signage with the restaurant name permanently displayed, and the name has to match the profile exactly.

Do that walk once, in daylight, before you open. Rejected videos are the single most common reason a restaurant sits unverified for weeks.

Once the profile is verified, you can properly manage the information customers see and use features such as review replies, photos and performance reporting.

And this is where the real work starts. A verified profile with the wrong hours, a dead website and a three-year-old menu is still a bad profile.

Get the basic information right first

Before worrying about posting specials or optimizing anything, make sure the boring stuff is correct.

Your restaurant name should match the real-world business name. Your address, phone number, website and hours should be current. Google specifically says businesses with complete and accurate information are easier to match to the right searches.

Restaurants can also set special hours for holidays and unusual days, along with additional hours for services such as delivery, takeout and pickup where those options apply.

That sounds simple until Christmas Eve arrives and Google tells somebody you are open when the doors are locked.

Treat your Google hours like the sign on the front door.

Choose the right restaurant category

Google asks you to select a primary business category, and that choice does more than describe the restaurant.

Google recommends choosing the most specific category that accurately describes the business. Categories can also determine which Business Profile features become available to you. Restaurant categories are what unlock things such as menu items, online-order links and reservation links.

So do not pick categories because they sound useful for SEO. Pick the most accurate description of what the restaurant actually is. If Google offers a more specific category that genuinely fits your operation, use it.

Where should the Website button go?

To your real, current website.

That sounds obvious, but it is one of the first things we check when looking at a restaurant online. Search your restaurant the way a customer would. Tap Website. See where you land.

Is it the current website? Does it load properly on a phone? Can someone immediately find the menu, hours, address and ordering options? Or does Google still point to an old site, an abandoned page or somewhere that no longer represents the restaurant?

Your Business Profile reports website clicks as a separate metric, so this is not a decorative link. People actually use it to leave Google and learn more about the business.

If you own a website, Google should be sending customers somewhere you control and keep current.

Put your actual menu on Google

Restaurants have several ways to show a menu, and this is one of the most useful parts of the profile.

You can add a menu link that sends customers to your menu online. You can also build a structured menu directly inside the Business Profile, with sections, individual dishes, descriptions and prices, which can then appear under the restaurant's menu on Google.

Google also lets restaurants upload menu photos and PDFs. There is now an experimental feature that uses AI to turn a menu photo or PDF into structured menu items, picking up section names, dish names, descriptions and prices. Google asks owners to review what it generates for accuracy before publishing, which is worth taking seriously: a mis-read price becomes a public price.

There is another detail restaurant owners should know. Google can find menu information from more than one source. When it finds several, the profile lets you choose a preferred menu source. Google may also pick up menu information from your own website.

That makes keeping the menu on your own website accurate even more important than it looks.

Customers can upload menu photos too. If somebody photographed your menu two years ago and the prices have changed, that old photo can still be what people see.

Search your restaurant and look at the menu customers actually see. Do not assume it matches the one at the host stand.

Check where the Order Online button sends people

This deserves special attention.

Restaurants can manage food-ordering options through their Business Profile, including their own ordering links and eligible third-party providers. Google lets businesses set a preferred ordering provider and request removal of a specific third-party provider link.

Google can also receive ordering-provider information from third parties that say they have an authorized relationship with the restaurant. That is how links you never set up can appear. The way ordering options show also varies by country and region.

Translation: look at your own restaurant on Google like a customer and press the button.

Where does Order Online go? Is that the platform you prefer? Is pickup going to the right place? Is delivery? Are there old or unwanted providers listed?

If you have a direct ordering page you would rather customers use, configure the profile accordingly instead of assuming Google worked it out for you. This matters commercially, not just cosmetically: the difference between a customer ordering through your own channel and through a third-party marketplace usually shows up in what you keep from the ticket.

This is one of those five-minute checks that can matter every day.

Add reservations and waitlists if you use them

Restaurants that take reservations can have booking functionality connected to their Business Profile. Google's restaurant tools support bookings and, for eligible restaurants, waitlists through participating providers. If you already use a compatible booking system you can connect it; otherwise you can look at the providers Google supports.

If reservations matter to your restaurant, check the public profile and make sure the booking path is current, exactly like you would check your ordering link.

A button that exists but sends people somewhere broken is worse than no button at all.

Add the social profiles you actually use

Google Business Profiles support links to social accounts including Facebook, Instagram, TikTok, YouTube, X, LinkedIn and Pinterest. You can add one link per platform, so one Facebook and one Instagram, not two of either.

For most independent restaurants, Instagram and Facebook are the obvious starting point. Add the accounts you genuinely keep up. An abandoned account linked from your profile is a worse signal than no link.

There is also a newer wrinkle worth knowing: Google says posts about events, deals or special offers from linked social profiles may also show on the Business Profile. This is available only in select regions and languages, so treat it as a bonus rather than something to plan around.

Your photos should answer one question: do I want to eat there?

Google lets verified businesses add a logo, cover image, photos and short videos to their profiles.

Google's own guidance on images is mostly about honesty: photos should be in focus, well lit, and free of significant alterations or heavy filters. The image should represent reality.

Our advice, from looking at a lot of restaurant listings, goes further. Show the front of the building so first-time customers know what they are looking for. Show the dining room. Show the food people actually order, not only the one dish that photographs best. Show the bar if the bar matters. Show the patio if you have one. Show seasonal dishes while they are actually available.

One useful thing to know: choosing a cover image does not guarantee Google will always show it first. Google says that in some cases selecting a cover photo will not make it the first image customers see.

That is another reason the whole photo library should be good, not just one hero shot.

Fill in the restaurant details people actually care about

Business Profile attributes let restaurants communicate details that otherwise force customers to hunt around or call.

Depending on your category and location, those can include things such as outdoor seating, Wi-Fi, payment options and accessibility features.

If your patio is open, say so. If the entrance is wheelchair accessible, fill it in accurately, and only if it is true. If there is an option Google gives you that answers a question customers regularly ask the staff, use it.

Small pieces of information add up to a much more useful listing.

Yes, restaurants can post directly to Google

Your Google listing does not have to sit there untouched between menu changes.

Businesses can create updates, offers and events that appear through the Business Profile on Search and Maps. Posts can include photos, video and action buttons that lead to things such as ordering, booking or more information.

That gives a restaurant plenty of obvious uses: a Friday fish special, Mother's Day hours, a new brunch menu, live music Saturday, a limited-time pizza, a hiring announcement.

Two practical details. Posts can be scheduled in advance, and can be set to repeat on a schedule, which is genuinely useful for a weekly special you already know about. And posts without a defined date range are archived after about six months.

You do not need to turn Google into another full-time social network. But an active restaurant should not look abandoned there either.

Ask for reviews, but do it correctly

Google encourages businesses to ask customers for reviews, and gives you a direct review link and QR code you can share.

What you should not do is pay for them. Google's policy explicitly prohibits offering incentives such as payment, discounts, or free goods and services in exchange for posting a review, or for revising or removing a negative one. Reviews collected that way can be removed.

A simple request is enough. Put the review QR code somewhere appropriate. Add the link to a follow-up message if you already communicate with customers. Ask happy regulars.

Then respond. Verified businesses can publicly reply to reviews, and short, specific, conversational replies land better than turning every response into another advertisement.

Reviews are not only a reputation feature. Google counts review count and score among the things that contribute to prominence in local results, so this is one area where good service and local search overlap naturally.

Is Google Business Profile free, or do I need to buy ads?

The Business Profile itself is free.

You do not need to buy Google Ads to create, claim or maintain your restaurant's listing. Ads are a separate, optional way to pay for additional exposure.

Google is also unusually direct about this: there is no way to request or pay for a better local ranking. Local results are determined mainly by relevance, distance and prominence. Complete, accurate information helps Google understand relevance. Distance is how far you are from the person searching. Prominence is how well known the business is, which is where things like reviews come in.

Before spending money pushing a half-finished listing, finish the listing.

Check what people are actually doing on your profile

Once your restaurant is verified, Google gives you performance information about how people find and interact with the Business Profile.

Depending on which features apply to your restaurant, that can include the searches people used to find you, profile views, calls, direction requests, website clicks, menu clicks and bookings.

This is worth checking because it gets you out of guessing. If people are finding you through searches you did not expect, you have learned something about how customers describe you. If hundreds of people are opening the menu, that menu deserves real attention. If customers are constantly requesting directions, make sure the exterior photo and location information are excellent.

Your Google listing is not just something to fill out once. It is one of the places where customers tell you what they are looking for.

A once-a-month Google check for restaurant owners

You do not need to spend hours on this. Once a month, and any time something important changes, open your public listing and check it the same way a customer would.

  1. Search the restaurant name on both Google Search and Maps. Confirm the name, category, address, phone number and normal hours, and check special or holiday hours when relevant.
  2. Tap the Website button and make sure the destination is current and works well on a phone.
  3. Open the menu. Check the link, the prices, the dishes and any menu photos customers can see.
  4. Tap the ordering options and confirm pickup and delivery go where you want them to go.
  5. Test reservation or waitlist links if you use them.
  6. Look through the first photos a customer sees, and add newer food, interior or exterior images when needed.
  7. Check your restaurant attributes and update anything that changed.
  8. Publish an update, offer or event when there is something genuinely useful to announce.
  9. Read recent reviews, reply where appropriate, and keep your review-request link handy.
  10. Open Performance and look at the searches, menu activity, website clicks, calls, directions and other interactions Google makes available.

That is it.

Google already gives restaurants a surprisingly capable storefront inside Search and Maps. Most of the important tools are free. The advantage comes from actually using them, and from making sure the information customers see matches the restaurant they are about to walk into.
